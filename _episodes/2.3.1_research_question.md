To close off this course, we would like to give you the opportunity to **design and work on your own research project**. 
Viromics is a new field, the virosphere is huge, and there is a LOT of data, so there are more unanswered than answered questions. 
Looking back on the course, are there things you are wondering about or want more time to work on? 
For inspiration, you could refine the ideas below or look in the literature. 
The important point to start your project is to **have a clear hypothesis**. Afterwards, make a bullet point plan of how you will be able to address it (methods), including any required statistics.    

How to make a clear, well-based hypothesis? **Start with a specific question**, which is neither too broad, now too narrow. An example: does adding the target phage and host (treatment *a*: with target phage and host) affect the virome community (treatment *b*: without target phage and host)? Afterwards, spend some time on **literature search** (no more than an hour) to understand what is already known about the topic and identify any knowledge gaps. If necessary, refine your question after the literature search. Then, make a list of **bullet points with methods** (no more than four). Finally, **discuss your ideas with a teacher** to make sure the project makes sense and is feasible within the time frame.   

Our suggestions of projects were all inspired by the experiments done in the wet lab in the module "Viromics - Virome isolation and sequencing":    

- Calculate the abundance of the target phage of the wet lab experiments over time and compare different treatments
- Predict bacterial hosts and compare them to the 16S sequencing of the bacteria
- Compare the phylogenetic trees of two different proteins found in the same contigs during this module
- Can we track the target phage/host in the complex community? When is it more/less abundant? 
- What kinds of mutations do we observe in the target phage/host? Any recombination with other community members?
- What differences do we observe in the other phages in the virome when target phage/host are added?

# Documentation

Do not forget to document the development of the project in your lab book. Write down your hypothesis, any relevant papers, methods, databases, etc.
Below are points that should be included in your documentation:

- A brief background on the topic
- A workflow for the steps you plan to take
- What will be the inputs and outputs?
- Which programs do you plan to use?

# Presentation  

The points below are what we expect to see in your final presentation:

- Hypothesis and brief information on the literature 
- Figure or bullet point list of methods
- A rationale for why you choose those methods
- What is the input
- What did you get as output (table(s) and/or figure(s))
- Interpretation of the output
  - what does the data tell you?
  - Does it answer your initial question? It does not have to. In a negative case, can you say what you might do differently next time?
- Limitations
- Things you found particularly interesting
- Any lessons learnt

> ## Exercise
> 1. Create a clear, well-based hypothesis, and discuss it with teachers and fellow students.
>    Often your initial idea can be further refined based on discussions with others.
> 2. Make sure you check the literature
>    Find at least one paper that is closely related to your project, and use it/them to refine your question.
> 3. Methods: Make a plan for tackling your question(s).
>    Think about data sources, bioinformatic methods, possible outcomes, expectations, backup/follow-up plans, hypotheses, and interpretation.
